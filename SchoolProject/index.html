Propaganda Movement

The Propaganda Movement was a reform and national consciousness
movement that arose among young Filipino expatriates in the late 19th century.

These students, the sons of wealthy families, were sent to Europe to study,
where they were exposed to more liberal ideas and developed a sense of Filipino identity.

Role of La Solidaridad

La Solidaridad served as the official mouthpiece of the Propaganda Movement, providing Filipino
ilustrados with a platform to campaign for peaceful reforms in the Philippines. Published in Spain
to bypass colonial censorship, the newspaper targeted Spanish liberals with demands for equal civil rights,
parliamentary representation, and an end to friar abuses. Key figures like José Rizal, Marcelo H. del Pilar,
and Graciano López Jaena used its pages to defend Filipino dignity, debunk racial stereotypes, and forge a unified
national identity.Though its ultimate failure to secure reform led to its closure in 1895, the paper’s rejection
convinced many that peaceful channels were exhausted, directly sparking the Katipunan revolution for complete independence.

Exposing Colonial and Friar Abuses

Through articles in La Solidaridad and satirical pamphlets such as Del Pilar's Dasalan at Tocsohan, the reformists documented real world misrule exposing friar corruption, excessive taxation, and forced labor to the Spanish public and government.

Demanding Legal and Political Reforms

Journalists published structured proposals advocating for peaceful, systemic changes rather than violence specifically demanding legal equality between Filipinos and Spaniards, freedom of speech, and seats for Filipino delegates in the Spanish parliament

Defending Filipino Dignity and Capability

Through historical and analytical essays such as Rizal's "The Indolence of the Filipino", the writers systematically disproved Spanish claims that Filipinos were naturally lazy or inferior, demonstrating that Filipinos were educated and fully capable of civic leadership.

Awakening a Shared Filipino National Identity

Even though reformist writings were banned in the Philippines, smuggled issues of La Solidaridad, novels, and pamphlets were passed around secret reading circles, unifying regional populations into a single, conscious Filipino nation.
